Scaling a POS for owner-operated businesses across seven markets

Flatpay's product spans PAY, POS, mPOS and a merchant portal across DK, FI, DE, IT, FR, UK and NL. The work below traces two of the value streams that shaped the 2026 roadmap: expanding into new geographies and unlocking the product for more complex merchants, from research to shipped design.

Flatpay KPIs

    % of new-market POS merchants reaching target TPV within 21 days of installation.

  • 02

    Conversion Unlocks

    P50 / P95 time from onboarding to a merchant's first live sale across core flows.

Researching new markets, then designing for what they actually need

Geo Expansion is Flatpay's primary growth lever: every new market needs to activate quickly and reach meaningful transaction volume fast. To get there, I ran in-depth merchant research in two markets ahead of launch, the UK and the Netherlands, and delivered the two reports below.

  • 01

    Research · Netherlands

    2025 NL UXR Report

    Part of the broader Geo Expansion research portfolio, establishing how Dutch merchants run their businesses, what payment habits and POS expectations look like locally, and where Flatpay's current product would need to flex ahead of a launch.

  • 02

    Research · United Kingdom

    2025 UK PMF Report

    Three days of cold, in-person merchant visits across Fulham Road, Hammersmith, North End Road, Covent Garden and Soho: interviews, observations and photo evidence with F&B, retail and service businesses that fit Flatpay's ICP.

    Finding: PAY fits the UK market as-is. POS fits smaller venues without table service, but to compete for venues with table service, Flatpay needed service charge handling, clock-in/clock-out, and an order/transaction history tab.

Two of the three gaps from the UK research have already shipped

The service charge finding moved quickly from research to feature: weighed against the launch-readiness matrix, designed end to end for Portal and POS, then used as the forcing function to rethink the POS's catch-all "all actions" menu, the structure that had been holding five growing markets inside one unchanged kebab menu since the product's earliest days. Staff identity followed soon after, laying the foundation the clock-in/clock-out tracking from the same UK finding depends on.

  • The earlier screen had no concept of a service charge, and cart actions lived in a separate screen, disconnected from the order itself.
  • The shipped version keeps those actions directly attached to the active order instead of hiding them behind a separate screen that feels disconnected.
  • The wider cart view gives the order list itself more room, keeping it readable while it's being built, and the 12.5% now appears automatically in the total.
  • That, in turn, frees up extra space for the register view, and the menu is built to scale: market-specific actions can be added to it later without reshaping the layout.
  • One login used to cover an entire shift, so a sale, a discount or a refund couldn't be traced back to the person who made it.
  • The shipped design keeps identity visible without adding friction: a small avatar in the corner of the register always shows who's signed in.
  • Tapping it opens the account menu with the full name and a one-tap Switch user control beside it.
  • That's the identity layer that clock-in/clock-out tracking, the second item on the UK list, needed to exist before it could be built.

Designing modifiers without making the POS harder to use

Modifiers let a merchant capture how a customer wants something ("no onions," "extra cheese," "medium rare") without bloating the menu or the order ticket. It's one of the most-requested features blocking Flatpay from serving more complex merchants, and the foundation for online ordering integrations with platforms like Uber Eats and Wolt.

Design process

  1. 01

    Merchant visits

    Visited three Copenhagen merchants who needed the feature, to validate scope assumptions, e.g. confirming negative values and quantities weren't actually needed for v1.

  2. 02

    Competitor benchmark

    Mapped how Toast, Lightspeed, Square and Tebi structure modifier groups, settings and POS flows, to ground the design in patterns merchants already know.

  3. 03

    Pattern-based design

    Designed the Portal and POS flows by extending Flatpay's existing patterns: keeping setup and checkout familiar rather than introducing new mental models.

  4. 04

    AI prototype

    Built an interactive prototype of the Portal setup flow to pressure-test the concept early: see it live in the embed below.

  5. 05

    Validation visit

    Returned to merchants with the PM and a developer. Key learning: "min/max selection" language wasn't clear; more merchant-accessible copy tested better, even at the cost of added complexity.

The phrase that didn't survive contact with merchants

The first pass framed selection rules the way the team thought about them internally: a minimum and a maximum to configure. In the validation visit, merchants stalled on it; they didn't think in ranges, they thought in plain yes-or-no questions. Does the cashier have to pick one? Can they pick more than one? The shipped version asks exactly that, even though it costs a little more screen space than the original dropdowns.

  • "Minimum selection of 1" became Required, with a line explaining that the cashier must choose before continuing.
  • "Maximum selection" became Allow multiple selections, a single switch a merchant can read at a glance.
  • Same underlying rules, rebuilt around how merchants actually talk about their menus.
